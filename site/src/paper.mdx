<Section id="abstract" num={2} kicker="Abstract" title="Satisfy a deployment-time constraint without leaving the policy">

A flow-matching policy deployed in the field must often satisfy a constraint that was absent from its demonstrations, such as avoiding an obstacle it has never seen. Retraining for every new constraint is impractical, so the constraint must be enforced at inference time. Enforcing it must not discard what the policy learned: an action the policy would rarely sample may avoid the obstacle yet fail the task. We therefore want actions that are both <i>safe</i>, satisfying the constraint, and <i>plausible</i>, remaining likely under the policy.

Gradient guidance, the most common approach, steers noisy states, so it must evaluate its cost on the policy's running estimate of the clean action; when the policy is uncertain between passing left or right of an obstacle, this estimate is their average and can pass straight through it. We instead build on rejection and projection. Rejection redraws actions until one is safe: every output is plausible, but the wait is intractable when safe actions are rare. Projection moves an action to the nearest safe one: it is efficient, but the result need not be plausible.

Our key insight is that flow-matching sampling lets us combine the two, keeping projection's efficiency and rejection's plausibility. <span class="cs">CS-Pi</span>, our draw-project-renoise sampler, projects each draw to the nearest safe action, adds noise back scaled to how far the projection moved it, and re-runs the unmodified policy until a draw is safe. Replacing only the projection step extends <span class="cs">CS-Pi</span> to continuous costs. Across a 2D diagnostic, a 7-DoF shelf pick-and-place, an orientation preference, and a zero-shot Franka deployment, <span class="cs">CS-Pi</span> reaches 81% safe success on the shelf and 55% on hardware, against 55% and 40% for projection, while staying plausible.

</Section>

<Section id="idea" num={4} kicker="The idea" title="Four ways to enforce a constraint, one that keeps the policy">

Rejection and projection act only on the <i>final</i> sampled action rather than guiding the intermediate noise levels. Flow-matching sampling lets us combine them, keeping projection's efficiency and rejection's plausibility: when a projected action is implausible, we need not take rejection's drastic step of restarting from pure noise. We can instead resample from a <i>reduced</i> noise level around the projection, so the policy returns a safe and plausible action near it.

<Section id="method" num={5} kicker="Method" title="Draw, project, renoise to n = k/(k+δ), re-run">

**Projection as a resampling target.** The projection a<sub>c</sub> = 𝒫(â) of the current clean draw, which we call the <i>anchor</i>, is cheap to compute, safe by construction, and closer than â to the safe actions the policy is likely to draw. It tells us <i>where</i> the next draw should concentrate, without executing it. The idealized target biases the policy toward the anchor while preserving its structure: q(a) ∝ π<sub>θ</sub>(a) · 𝒩(a; a<sub>c</sub>, σ²I).

**The residual sets the width.** We set σ = δ/k from the projection residual δ = ‖â − a<sub>c</sub>‖, how far the projection moved the draw. A small δ means the current draw was nearly safe, so plausible actions likely lie near the anchor and the next draw should concentrate there. A large δ means the anchor may sit where the policy assigns vanishing mass, so the next draw should be loose enough to give a different policy mode (passing right of an obstacle rather than left) a chance. The coefficient k is the only hyperparameter, and it slides between the two classical extremes: k → 0 recovers rejection, k → ∞ recovers projection.

**Partial renoising realises the target.** Sampling q exactly is intractable, since the policy density is defined only through the ODE. Instead we renoise the anchor to time n = k/(k+δ) and re-denoise from there. This ties the amount of noise to σ, since (1 − n)/n = δ/k = σ: a larger residual adds more noise. We pass each new draw back through the loop until it lands within τ of 𝒞; on average each draw lands closer to the safe set than the last.

**Soft preferences as continuous costs.** Deployment also brings preferences, behaviours to favour rather than boundaries to clear, such as keeping the gripper near-vertical while carrying a full cup. <span class="cs">CS-Pi</span> uses the projection only to produce an anchor for the next draw, so for a continuous cost L that anchor comes instead from the proximal operator prox<sub>βL</sub>(â) = argmin<sub>a′</sub> L(a′) + ‖a′ − â‖² / 2β, which trades reducing L against staying close to the current draw; the rest of the algorithm is unchanged. Hard constraints and costs then compose by applying the operators in sequence, with their order encoding priority: pull toward the preferred orientation first, then project onto the safe set, so the constraint takes precedence. A gradient method would have to merge both into a single objective.

**Optional terminal projection.** If no draw lies within τ of 𝒞 after K<sub>max</sub> iterations, <span class="cs">CS-Pi</span> can project the last draw and execute the result, trading plausibility for safety when safety must come first. Even then the loop pays off: in simulation, the executed actions are more likely under the policy than a typical one-shot projection.

<Section id="results" num={8} kicker="Results">
  <Fragment slot="title"><span class="cs">CS-Pi</span> departs from the policy only as far as the constraint forces</Fragment>

For each sampler we measure two things: whether it satisfies the constraint, and whether its samples are plausible. The primary metric is <i>safe success</i>, the fraction of rollouts that satisfy the constraint and complete the task. Plausibility is measured by logL, the mean log-likelihood the policy assigns to a method's actions (higher is more plausible). We compare against gradient guidance, Trust Sampling, rejection, projection, the unconstrained policy, and two control-barrier-function samplers (SafeDiffuser and SafeFlowMatcher), each reported at the best value of its main hyperparameters; <span class="cs">CS-Pi</span> has a single hyperparameter, k, and is robust across a broad range of it. NFE counts flow-network forward passes and is not comparable across benchmarks. The <span class="cs">CS-Pi</span> row is highlighted; best per column is bold.

### 2D Avoidance: seeing the whole distribution

Demonstrations reach the goal along distinct paths, so the policy is multimodal, mirroring manipulation data where demonstrations of one task take different routes. Because the action space is two-dimensional, we can plot every sampler's entire output distribution, and measure plausibility by how many rollouts stay inside the demonstration support. At deployment we impose obstacles the policy never saw, in six scenarios of varying difficulty.

### Composing a hard constraint with a soft preference

A feasibility set cannot express a preference among feasible actions. On the same 2D environment we add to the obstacle constraint a soft preference toward a height y<sub>ref</sub> = 0.7. <span class="cs">CS-Pi</span> applies the two in sequence by priority, whereas the baselines must fold them into one weighted cost; only <span class="cs">CS-Pi</span> satisfies both.
